\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cut a planarian in half and each half regrows a whole animal. The body comes
back. Whether anything the animal \emph{knew} comes back is a separate question,
and for a distributed system it is a sharper one than it first appears: if
knowledge is not stored anywhere in particular, then destroying a region does not
delete a fact, it perturbs a computation that was being carried out everywhere at
once.

Neural cellular automata make that question askable. A single shared local update
rule, applied by every cell to its own neighbourhood, grows a target morphology
from a seed and regrows it after tissue loss
\citep{mordvintsev2020growing}. There is no component to remove --- damage
removes computation, and the system's response is to recompute rather than to
restore from backup. What has never been asked of such a system is whether it can
\emph{invent and hold a convention}: a mapping from things in the world to
symbols, agreed with a partner, that could survive or fail to survive the
destruction and regrowth of the substrate computing it.

We ask exactly that. Two neural cellular automata grow from single seed cells and
play a Lewis referential game \citep{lewis1969convention}: a sender observes a
referent and emits a discrete symbol through a parameter-free consensus readout
over its own cells; a receiver, which observes nothing else, must identify the
referent. Then we cut the sender open, hold the receiver frozen and healthy, and
watch what comes back.

Freezing the receiver is what makes the measurement clean. An unchanged partner
is a fixed semantic reference frame, so any drop in task accuracy is the sender's
protocol drifting away from the meaning its partner was trained to read, rather
than the two of them renegotiating.

\paragraph{Thesis.} \emph{Regeneration restores the body and the channel
capacity, but not the convention.} Morphological and semantic recovery are
different events, and plotting them on the same axis separates them.

Stated carefully, the claim is that the event
%
\begin{center}
  \emph{body recovered} $\wedge$ \emph{still speaking a language} $\wedge$
  \emph{no longer mutually intelligible}
\end{center}
%
occurs, and occurs at a measurable rate. The middle clause is what makes this
substantive rather than trivial. Without it the claim reduces to ``RGBA recovered
but the hidden channels did not,'' which is a fact about linear algebra --- four
of twenty-eight channels were supervised and the rest were not. Requiring that
the regenerated organism still carries as much information about the referent as
it did before rules out the deflationary reading that damage simply made it
stupid.

\paragraph{The measurement problem is the second contribution.} There is no
existing definition of semantic recovery to borrow. Work on neural cellular
automata measures recovery as pixel distance to a target morphology or as
downstream task score; work on emergent communication has never needed the notion
at all, because its agents are fixed-architecture networks that cannot be
structurally damaged. Section~\ref{sec:protocol} supplies four operationally
distinct recoveries --- morphological, behavioural, computational, semantic ---
and a decomposition of the last into whether the system still speaks \emph{a}
language, whether it speaks the \emph{same} one, and how much of any difference
is pure relabeling.

That machinery is not decoration. The most dangerous objection to the whole
enterprise is that our readout is a mean over living cells, so deleting cells
changes it even if every survivor computes exactly what it did before. We answer
that twice: with a proposition showing that under full per-cell consensus,
deletion \emph{provably cannot} flip the symbol
(Proposition~\ref{prop:deletion}), and with an exact frozen-state control that
separates mechanical pooling shift from genuine change in cell computation at
zero additional cost (Section~\ref{sec:mechanical}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item The first study of emergent communication in which the communicating
  agents are self-organizing, damage-regenerating substrates
  (Section~\ref{sec:task}).
\item A measurement protocol for \emph{semantic} recovery, including the
  struct/align/relabel decomposition, the mechanical-versus-dynamical control,
  and a confound analysis with a named control for each alternative explanation
  (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\item A parameter-free consensus readout with a proved deletion-invariance
  property and a mechanical $1/|\alive|$ bound on any single cell's influence,
  verified against a matched negative control (Section~\ref{sec:readout}).
\item A direct measurement of how far a morphology-trained cellular automaton
  transports an injected signal --- a strong negative result that reshaped the
  design, and one we expect to be useful to anyone building tasks on this
  substrate (Section~\ref{sec:results-probe}).
\end{enumerate}
